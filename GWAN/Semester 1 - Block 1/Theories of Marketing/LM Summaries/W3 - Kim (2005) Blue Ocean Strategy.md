## Executive Summary

The prevailing approach to corporate strategy is rooted in military-style competition, focusing on outperforming rivals within defined industry boundaries—a space termed "red oceans." Research indicates that while most business launches (86%) occur within these red oceans, they deliver only 39% of total profits. In contrast, "blue oceans"—untapped market spaces where competition is irrelevant—account for only 14% of launches but generate 61% of total profits.

The "Blue Ocean Strategy" challenges the structuralist view that market conditions are fixed. Instead, it proposes a "reconstructionist" view, where companies break the traditional value-cost trade-off by simultaneously driving costs down and value up. By using analytical tools like the Strategy Canvas and the Four Actions Framework, organizations can move away from benchmarking competitors and toward creating new demand from noncustomers, as demonstrated by the rapid global success of brands like [yellow tail] wine.

## The Dual Universe of Market Space

Market space is divided into two distinct environments: red oceans and blue oceans.

### Red Oceans: The Known Market Space

Red oceans represent all industries in existence today. Strategy in this space is characterized by:

- **Defined Boundaries:** Industry rules and competitive limits are accepted and understood.
- **Zero-Sum Competition:** Companies strive to outperform rivals to capture a greater share of existing demand. As the space becomes crowded, profit and growth prospects diminish.
- **Commoditization:** Intense competition turns the water "bloody" as products become indistinguishable and price wars erupt.
- **Structuralist Roots:** This view, rooted in industrial organization economics, suggests that market structure is given and firms must build defensible positions within it.

### Blue Oceans: The Unknown Market Space

Blue oceans denote industries not currently in existence. They are characterized by:

- **Untapped Demand:** These spaces offer opportunities for highly profitable growth by creating new demand rather than fighting for existing customers.
- **Irrelevant Competition:** Because the "rules of the game" have yet to be set, traditional competitive advantages are moot.
- **Market Reconstruction:** Most blue oceans are created from within red oceans by expanding existing industry boundaries through a shift in strategic focus.

## The Performance Imperative: Evidence for Blue Oceans

A study of 108 business launches across various companies highlights the disproportionate impact of blue ocean creation on profitability and growth.

|   |   |   |
|---|---|---|
|Metric|Red Ocean (Line Extensions)|Blue Ocean (New Markets)|
|**Percentage of Launches**|86%|14%|
|**Revenue Impact**|62%|38%|
|**Profit Impact**|39%|61%|

Despite the clear profit advantages, companies often remain tethered to red oceans due to an overabundance of analytical tools designed for competition rather than creation. Current market forces, including accelerated technological advances, globalization, and shrinking populations in developed markets, are increasing the urgency to move into blue oceans as traditional "monopoly havens" and niche markets disappear.

## The Reconstructionist View of Strategy

The fundamental difference between red and blue ocean strategies lies in their underlying economic theories.

- **Structuralist View (Red Ocean):** Assumes industry conditions are fixed. Firms must choose between **differentiation** (high value/high cost) or **low cost** (reasonable value/low cost).
- **Reconstructionist View (Blue Ocean):** Assumes market boundaries exist only in managers' minds. It focuses on the **demand side** rather than the supply side. The strategic aim is to break the value-cost trade-off by creating new rules.

### Value Innovation

Blue ocean strategy is achieved only when the entire system of a company’s utility, price, and cost activities is aligned. It is more than innovation; it is a whole-system approach that integrates functional and operational activities to drive costs down while driving value up for buyers.

## Analytical Frameworks and Tools

To make the creation of blue oceans systematic and actionable, several diagnostic and action frameworks are utilized.

### The Strategy Canvas

The strategy canvas is a diagnostic tool that captures the current state of play in the known market space.

- **Horizontal Axis:** Identifies the factors the industry currently competes on and invests in.
- **Vertical Axis:** Captures the offering level buyers receive across these factors.
- **Value Curve:** A graphic depiction of a company’s relative performance. Converging value curves among competitors signal a company is "caught in the red ocean."

### The Four Actions Framework

To reconstruct buyer value and create a new value curve, companies must answer four key questions:

1. **Eliminate:** Which factors that the industry takes for granted should be eliminated? (Focuses on factors that no longer add value).
2. **Reduce:** Which factors should be reduced well below the industry’s standard? (Addresses over-designed products that increase costs).
3. **Raise:** Which factors should be raised well above the industry’s standard? (Uncovers compromises forced on customers).
4. **Create:** Which factors should be created that the industry has never offered? (Discovers new sources of value).

### The Eliminate-Reduce-Raise-Create Grid

This tool complements the Four Actions Framework by pushing companies to act on all four fronts simultaneously. It provides four immediate benefits:

- Pursues differentiation and low cost simultaneously.
- Flags companies focused only on "raising and creating," which leads to over-engineering.
- Is easily understood by managers at all levels.
- Forces scrutiny of every factor the industry competes on.

## Case Study: The [yellow tail] Breakthrough

In the late 1990s, the U.S. wine industry was a red ocean characterized by intense competition, price pressure, and stagnant demand. Casella Wines used the Four Actions Framework to create [yellow tail], transforming wine from a "refined beverage" into a "social drink accessible to everyone."

### Strategic Profile of [yellow tail]

Using the Eliminate-Reduce-Raise-Create Grid, Casella Wines reconstructed the industry's value elements:

|   |   |
|---|---|
|Action|Factors Addressed|
|**Eliminate**|Enological terminology, aging quality, above-the-line marketing.|
|**Reduce**|Wine complexity, range of wines, vineyard prestige.|
|**Raise**|Price versus budget wines, retail store involvement.|
|**Create**|Easy drinking, ease of selection, fun and adventure.|

### Outcomes

- **New Demand:** [yellow tail] pulled in "noncustomers"—beer and cocktail drinkers—who previously found wine intimidating or pretentious.
- **Growth:** Within two years, it became the fastest-growing brand in Australian and U.S. wine history and the #1 imported wine in the U.S.
- **Operational Simplicity:** By offering only two wines initially (a red and a white) and using the same bottle shape for both, Casella streamlined its business model and minimized warehouse inventory.

## Evaluating Strategic Viability

An effective blue ocean strategy exhibits three complementary qualities:

1. **Focus:** The company does not diffuse its efforts across all factors of competition.
2. **Divergence:** The strategic profile stands apart from the industry's average (the "me-too" strategy).
3. **Compelling Tagline:** The strategy is clear and easy to communicate (e.g., [yellow tail] as a "fun and simple wine for every day").

### Indicators of Strategic Failure

- **Convergence:** Value curves that mirror competitors indicate a struggle for market share based on cost or quality.
- **Over-delivery without Payback:** High levels of offering across all factors without a corresponding increase in market share or profit suggest oversupplying customers.
- **Incoherent Strategy:** A "zigzag" value curve suggests independent substrategies and organizational silos.
- **Strategic Contradictions:** Offering high levels on one factor while ignoring supporting factors (e.g., an easy-to-use website with slow speeds).
- **Inside-Out Perspective:** Using operational jargon to label competing factors rather than terms buyers understand.
