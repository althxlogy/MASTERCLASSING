# Rewarding Product Retention: 

## Executive Summary

This briefing document examines research into a novel promotional strategy for online retailing: the "keep reward." Online retailers currently face a paradox where lenient return policies—essential for driving initial orders—simultaneously encourage high return rates and substantial operational costs. This research proposes that adding a keep reward (incentives provided when a customer chooses not to return a product) to a conventional lenient policy can effectively increase product retention without deterring initial purchases.

Key findings indicate that keep rewards are most effective in high-return industries like apparel and are particularly powerful among frequent online shoppers. For frequent shoppers, the reward acts as positive reinforcement, driven by a high intention to repurchase. Conversely, the strategy can be counterproductive for infrequent shoppers, who may perceive the reward as a persuasion tactic, leading to customer reactance.

## 1. The Context of Online Return Management

Online purchase decisions occur in two distinct stages, each presenting different challenges for retailers:

- **Decision Stage 1 (Order Intention):** Customers decide whether to order. This stage is marked by high uncertainty as customers cannot physically inspect product quality.
- **Decision Stage 2 (Keep/Return Decision):** Customers inspect the delivered items and make the final purchase decision.

### Comparison of Existing Return Policies

|   |   |   |   |
|---|---|---|---|
|Policy Type|Strategy|Impact on Stage 1 (Order)|Impact on Stage 2 (Keep)|
|**Lenient Policy**|Liberal handling, free reshipment, generous time limits.|**High:** Reduces risk and increases customer certainty.|**Low:** Encourages returns if expectations are not met.|
|**Restrictive Policy**|Barriers such as reshipment fees.|**Low:** Deterrent; customers anticipate the cost of a "bad" decision.|**High:** Reduces returns due to associated costs/penalties.|

The research identifies the "lenient policy" as the current industry status quo, despite the significant costs associated with high return rates.

## 2. Theoretical Framework: Operant Conditioning

The study utilizes **Operant Conditioning** (Skinner, 1965) to explain customer behavior. This theory suggests that individuals adjust behavioral tendencies based on the consequences of their actions.

- **Positive Reinforcement:** Presenting positive consequences to increase desired behavior. A keep reward serves as reinforcement for the desired "keep" decision.
- **Continuous vs. Intermittent Reinforcement:** The study advocates for a **continuous schedule** (rewarding the behavior every time), as intermittent rewards in a marketing context can be perceived as a form of punishment, driving customers to competitors.
- **Punishment:** Presenting negative consequences to decrease undesired behavior. While restrictive policies use punishment (fees), they often result in customers avoiding the retailer entirely.

## 3. Qualitative Pilot Study: Managerial Feasibility

Interviews with 11 e-commerce executives (primarily in the apparel industry) established the groundwork for the keep reward strategy:

- **Current Metrics:** Average return rates among interviewed firms were 43%.
- **Feasibility:** Managers found the keep reward most feasible for **low- to mid-priced products**. They doubted that reasonably priced rewards could influence decisions on luxury items.
- **Optimal Reward Type:** "Free shipment for the next order" was identified as the most effective incentive. Rewards tied to future transactions are preferred over immediate discounts or gifts.
- **Targeted Situations:** Keep rewards were deemed most effective for "soft" return reasons, such as a customer simply not liking an item or having placed a multiple-item choice order.

## 4. Experimental Analysis of Keep Rewards

### Study 1: Impact in the Apparel Industry

Conducted with 217 participants using an apparel-based scenario, Study 1 tested the core hypothesis: **Keep intention under a lenient policy plus keep reward is higher than under a conventional lenient policy.**

- **Findings on Order Intention:** There was no statistically significant difference in order intention between the conventional lenient policy and the lenient policy plus keep reward. Both outperformed the restrictive policy.
- **Findings on Keep Intention:** The keep reward significantly increased the intention to keep products compared to the conventional lenient policy.
- **Conclusion:** The keep reward successfully improves the lenient policy by maintaining high sales volume (Stage 1) while reducing return volume (Stage 2).

### Study 2: Generalizability and Moderating Factors

Conducted with 138 participants using an electronics-based scenario, Study 2 examined why and for whom keep rewards work.

- **Industry Differences:** Unlike the apparel study, the general positive effect of the keep reward was not replicated for electronics. This is attributed to the lower baseline return sensitivity of the electronics industry (where body fit is not a factor).
- **Moderator: Online Shopping Frequency:** The effectiveness of the reward depends heavily on how often the customer shops online for that category.
- **Mediator: Repurchase Intention:** The effect of the reward is processed through the customer's intent to shop at the store again.

## 5. Strategic Mechanisms: Frequency and Reactance

The research highlights a critical divide in how different customer segments respond to keep rewards:

### Frequent Online Shoppers

- **Goal Proximity:** Frequent shoppers feel closer to the "goal" of the next purchase.
- **Response:** They view the reward (e.g., free shipping on the next order) as highly valuable because they intend to buy again soon. This increases their willingness to keep a product even if they feel slight dissonance (e.g., not liking it 100%).

### Infrequent Online Shoppers

- **Reactance:** Infrequent shoppers may perceive the reward as a subtle persuasion tactic or a "trap" to force more spending.
- **Response:** Because their repurchase intention is low, the reward holds little value. It may even trigger a "rebellious act" where the customer returns the item to assert their freedom of choice, leading to lower keep intentions than a standard lenient policy.

## 6. Managerial Recommendations

### Industry-Specific Implementation

- **High-Return Industries (e.g., Apparel):** Retailers should implement keep rewards broadly. The strategy reduces "unethical retail disposition" (e.g., fraudulent returns or wearing clothes once) and encourages "co-creation" behavior, where customers might adapt a product rather than return it.
- **Medium-Return Industries (e.g., Electronics):** Retailers should use customer data to segment their audience. Keep rewards should be offered exclusively to frequent shoppers.

### Operational Tactics

- **Communication:** Keep rewards should be communicated openly on the website (e.g., the welcome page) to build trust and signal a "keep management" culture rather than just "return management."
- **Loyalty Integration:** Retailers should incorporate keep rewards into existing loyalty programs (e.g., Amazon Prime). This allows for targeted rewarding without discriminating against infrequent shoppers.
- **Reward Design:** Focus on **delayed rewards** (applicable to future purchases). This not only encourages the customer to keep the current item but also secures a future transaction.
- **Price Point:** Focus on the **low- to mid-priced segment** where the relative value of a reward (like a €6 shipping fee) is significant compared to the product price.
