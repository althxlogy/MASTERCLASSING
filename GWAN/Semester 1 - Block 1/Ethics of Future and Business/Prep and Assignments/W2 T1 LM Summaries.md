# ALL W2 T1 Sources: Sustainability Briefing: Evolutionary Psychology, Corporate Responsibility, and Stakeholder Engagement

## Executive Summary

This briefing synthesizes research and frameworks regarding the psychological, corporate, and strategic dimensions of sustainability. The central challenge identified is a profound mismatch between evolved human nature—shaped by ancestral environments of scarcity and small nomadic bands—and the demands of a modern, technologically advanced world of seven billion people. To mitigate ecological and social degradation, strategies must leverage, rather than resist, five core human tendencies: self-interest, status-seeking, social imitation, shortsightedness, and a disregard for impalpable concerns.

From a corporate perspective, sustainability is increasingly defined as a macro-level necessity, balancing enterprise embeddedness within social, environmental, and economic systems. Modern corporations face rising pressure from "datafication" and the transgression of planetary boundaries, necessitating a shift from "business-as-usual" toward integrated Environmental, Social, and Governance (ESG) frameworks. Success in this landscape requires a robust, five-step stakeholder engagement process focused on building mutual trust rather than mere public relations.

## Detailed Analysis of Evolutionary Bases for Sustainable Behavior

Analysis of the provided research indicates that human behavior is driven by "ultimate" evolutionary causes that often operate nonconsciously. While modern technology has advanced rapidly, the human brain remains equipped with mechanisms adaptive for a Stone Age environment.

### Five Adaptive Tendencies and Strategic Implications

The research identifies five inherited tendencies that contribute to environmental exploitation and provides evolutionary-matched strategies for intervention:

|   |   |   |
|---|---|---|
|Ancestral Tendency|Theoretical Principle|Strategy Matched with Ancestral Tendencies|
|**Self-interest**|Kin Selection; Reciprocal Altruism|Highlight benefits to genetic kin; create small, dense social networks; use "reciprocity by proxy" (giving before asking).|
|**Relative Status**|Costly Signaling; Competitive Altruism|Publicize "green lists" to foster status competition; make proenvironmental acts visible; ensure green products signal high status/cost.|
|**Social Imitation**|Mimicry; Social Norms|Depict the high prevalence of desired behaviors; use social approval (e.g., "smiley faces") to reinforce conservation.|
|**Shortsightedness**|Life History Theory; Sexual Selection|Emphasize immediate consequences for the present generation; highlight environmental stability to encourage long-term thinking.|
|**Impalpable Concerns**|Sensory Mechanisms; Biophilia|Use visceral cues (smell, sight) to link behavior to consequences; tap into the innate "biophilia" or love of nature.|

### Key Evolutionary Insights

- **The Myth of the "Ecological Noble Savage":** Evidence suggests that traditional societies were not inherently conservationist; their low impact was due to low population density and lack of technology. Humans have a long history of producing ecological calamities.
- **Proximate vs. Ultimate Causes:** While people may act green for altruistic values (proximate), the underlying drive is often linked to reproductive fitness or reputation (ultimate).
- **The Mismatch Hypothesis:** Brain evolution takes millennia, while environment and technology change rapidly. Strategies like "guilt" or "counting calories" often fail because they work against evolved impulses to devour scarce, calorie-dense resources.
- **Competitive Environmentalism:** Status motives can be harnessed. For example, the Toyota Prius became a status symbol because its unique design made the owner's "conspicuous conservation" visible to others.

## Corporate Sustainability, Responsibility, and Ethics

The documentation outlines the evolving role of corporations in society, moving from purely economic entities to political and social actors embedded in global systems.

### Core Definitions and Levels of Analysis

Corporate engagement is categorized into three distinct but interrelated concepts:

1. **Corporate Sustainability (Macro Level):** Managing and balancing an enterprise's embeddedness in ecological, social, and economic systems to create long-term balance and stakeholder value.
2. **Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR) (Meso Level):** Integrating social, environmental, and philanthropic responsibilities into core business strategy and operations in cooperation with stakeholders.
3. **Business Ethics (Micro Level):** The study of business situations and decisions where issues of right and wrong are addressed; it serves as a moral foundation for all conduct.

### The ESGT Framework

The scope of sustainability issues is often organized into the ESGT framework, representing Environmental, Social, Governance, and Technology:

|   |   |
|---|---|
|Dimension|Examples of Key Issues|
|**Environmental**|Carbon emissions, water management, biodiversity, land use, raw material sourcing.|
|**Social**|Health and safety, diversity and inclusion, employee pay, union relations, poverty.|
|**Governance**|Corruption, executive pay, board diversity, stakeholder rights, lobbying.|
|**Technology**|Cybersecurity, fake news, data mining, bioengineering, surveillance.|

### Trends and Challenges in Corporate Sustainability

- **Planetary Boundaries:** Research shows four of nine planetary boundaries—climate, biodiversity, biogeochemical cycles, and land-system change—have already been transgressed.
- **The Digital Economy:** "Datafication" has increased transparency and public scrutiny of corporate misconduct, but also consumes significant energy (the Internet uses ~10% of world electricity).
- **The Regulatory Vacuum:** Multinational Corporations (MNCs) often operate in a "regulatory vacuum" where nation-states lack the power to regulate conduct across borders, leading to calls for stronger extraterritorial jurisdiction.
- **Carroll’s Pyramid of Responsibilities:** A foundational framework suggesting firms must meet responsibilities in a specific hierarchy:
    - **Economic:** Be profitable (The foundation).
    - **Legal:** Obey the law.
    - **Ethical:** Do what is right and fair.
    - **Philanthropic:** Be a good corporate citizen (Descretionary).

## The Five-Step Approach to Stakeholder Engagement

Modern stakeholder engagement is no longer a public relations exercise; it is a fundamental component of sustainability strategy and materiality assessments.

### BSR’s Five-Step Process

1. **Engagement Strategy:** Set the vision and level of ambition for future engagement; review past performance.
2. **Stakeholder Mapping:** Define criteria for identifying and prioritizing stakeholders; select appropriate engagement mechanisms.
3. **Preparation:** Focus on long-term goals, determine logistics, and set the "rules" for the engagement.
4. **Engagement:** Conduct the actual dialogue, ensuring equitable contributions and mitigating tensions while staying focused on priorities.
5. **Action Plan:** Identify opportunities from feedback, determine specific actions, and "close the feedback loop" by following up with stakeholders.

### The High-Stakes Environment of 2019

- **Connectivity and Social Networks:** Exponential growth in internet access allows unfiltered updates to reach millions in real time. Disputations about a company can be amplified globally, causing "whiplash" for firms.
- **Trust Deficit:** There is a widening trust gap between companies and stakeholders, necessitating an emphasis on building mutual trust rather than just managing communications.
- **Political Involvement:** Corporations are increasingly drawn into taking positions on sensitive issues (e.g., immigration, climate change) that were previously the domain of policymakers.
- **Global Frameworks:** Engagement is now guided by internationally accepted standards, including the UN Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), the Paris Agreement on Climate, and the UN Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights.
