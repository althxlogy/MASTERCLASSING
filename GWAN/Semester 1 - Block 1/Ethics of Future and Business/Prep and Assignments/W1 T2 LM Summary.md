# 201 Crane: Descriptive Ethical Theories in Business: A Briefing on Decision-Making

## Executive Summary

This document synthesizes key theories and research regarding how ethical decisions are actually made in the business environment. Moving beyond normative theories—which dictate what business people _should_ do—descriptive ethical theory seeks to explain why individuals act as they do, identifying the specific factors that influence both the process and outcomes of their decisions.

The core of ethical decision-making is shaped by the interaction between **individual factors** (traits, values, and cognitive development) and **situational factors** (organizational culture, reward systems, and the nature of the ethical issue itself). Analysis reveals that unethical behavior is rarely the result of a single "evil" individual; instead, it often emerges from complex institutional norms, social connections, and organizational pressures. Key findings include the prevalence of consequentialist reasoning (such as cost-benefit analysis) in corporate settings and the significant role that "moral framing" and "moral muteness" play in how managers perceive and discuss ethical dilemmas.

## Defining the Ethical Decision

Before a decision can be analyzed, it must be identified as having "moral status." A situation is characterized as an ethical decision based on three primary factors:

- **Significant Effects on Others:** The decision is likely to result in harms or benefits for parties beyond the self.
- **Availability of Choice:** Multiple alternative courses of action must be open to the decision-maker. Without choice, there is no ethical dilemma.
- **Perception by Others:** Regardless of whether the decision-maker views the act as ethical, if others (e.g., campaigners, colleagues, or the public) perceive it as such, the decision incurs ethical relevance.

## The Process of Ethical Decision-Making

While some modern perspectives emphasize "intuitionism" (quick moral intuitions) or "sentimentalism" (emotion-based mechanisms), the dominant framework in business ethics research remains the **rationalist perspective**.

### The Four-Stage Model

Introduced by James Rest, this model outlines the logical sequence individuals generally follow when responding to an ethical problem.

|   |   |
|---|---|
|Stage|Description|
|**1. Awareness**|Recognizing that a moral issue exists; also known as moral sensitivity.|
|**2. Judgement**|The actual evaluation or reasoning to determine the right course of action.|
|**3. Intent**|Establishing the motivation or determination to act on the moral judgement.|
|**4. Behaviour**|Implementing the decision through action; the final outward behavior.|

**Note on Stage Inconsistency:** Reaching one stage does not guarantee movement to the next. For instance, a salesperson may judge that lying to a customer is wrong (Stage 2) but, due to aggressive sales targets, may lack the intent to tell the truth (Stage 3) or fail to behave ethically (Stage 4).

## Individual Influences on Decision-Making

Individual influences are the unique traits and characteristics that employees bring to the organization.

### Demographic and Cultural Factors

- **Age and Gender:** Research is inconclusive. While some studies suggest women are more ethical or men are stricter in judgement, others find no difference. Gender is increasingly viewed as a complex construct involving socialization and role attitudes.
- **National and Cultural Characteristics:** Cultural "mental programming" significantly impacts ethical beliefs. Geert Hofstede identified six dimensions that explain these variations:
    - **Individualism/Collectivism:** Preference for autonomous action vs. group orientation.
    - **Power Distance:** Acceptance of hierarchical inequality.
    - **Uncertainty Avoidance:** Preference for rules and absolute truths.
    - **Masculinity/Femininity:** Valuing money/things vs. people/relationships.
    - **Long-term/Short-term Orientation:** Focus on future rewards vs. short-term results/social obligations.
    - **Indulgence:** The degree to which a society permits the gratification of human drives.

### Psychological Factors

- **Cognitive Moral Development (CMD):** Developed by Lawrence Kohlberg, this theory identifies three levels of moral reasoning:
    1. **Pre-conventional:** Focus on self-interest, rewards, and punishments.
    2. **Conventional:** Focus on meeting the expectations of others and social accord. (Most business managers operate at this level).
    3. **Post-conventional:** Autonomous decision-making based on universal principles of justice and rights.
- **Locus of Control:** This measures the extent to which an individual believes they control their life events. Those with an **internal** locus believe they shape their own fate and are generally more resistant to peer pressure. Those with an **external** locus attribute events to luck, fate, or the actions of others.

### Values, Integrity, and Moral Imagination

- **Personal Values:** Enduring beliefs that specific modes of conduct are preferable. While companies often seek "values congruency" (aligning employee and corporate values), this can be dangerous in professions where professional ethics may clash with market-oriented goals.
- **Personal Integrity:** Adherence to a consistent set of moral principles. This is often the driving force behind **whistleblowing**—the act of exposing an employer's ethical or legal violations.
- **Moral Imagination:** The creativity to see beyond the "rules of the game" and imagine a wide range of possible consequences and solutions to a dilemma.

## Situational Influences on Decision-Making

Evidence suggests that situational factors are often more influential than individual traits, as people may have "multiple ethical selves" that emerge in different contexts.

### Issue-Related Factors

The nature of the ethical issue itself changes the decision-making process.

- **Moral Intensity:** The relative importance of the issue to the decision-maker, determined by six factors: magnitude of consequences, social consensus, probability of effect, temporal immediacy, proximity, and concentration of effect.
- **Moral Framing:** The language used to describe a situation. Using moral terms (e.g., "stealing") prompts moral thinking, whereas "euphemistic language" (e.g., "facilitation payments" instead of bribes) can mask the ethical nature of an act.

### Context-Related Factors

- **Systems of Reward:** Employees are likely to do what they are rewarded for. If unethical behavior is unpunished or rewarded with bonuses/promotions, it is likely to be repeated.
- **Authority:** Subordinates tend to follow the explicit and implicit orders of their superiors, sometimes acting as "moral robots" who do not question instructions.
- **Bureaucracy:** Bureaucratic structures can suppress moral autonomy by:
    - **Instrumental Morality:** Focusing on the efficient achievement of goals rather than the goals' moral substance.
    - **Distancing:** Separating the decision-maker from the consequences of their actions (e.g., a manager in London ignoring labor conditions in Colombia).
    - **Denial of Moral Status:** Treating people or animals as "resources" or "units of production."

## Rationalizing Unethical Behaviour

When individuals engage in corrupt acts, they often use "rationalization tactics" to neutralize regret and justify their behavior to themselves and others.

|   |   |
|---|---|
|Strategy|Description|
|**Denial of Responsibility**|Claiming there was no other choice (e.g., "My arm is being twisted").|
|**Denial of Injury**|Claiming no one was really harmed.|
|**Denial of Victim**|Arguing that the violated party deserved what happened.|
|**Social Weighting**|Condemning the critics or making selective social comparisons (e.g., "Others are worse than we are").|
|**Appeal to Higher Loyalties**|Claiming the act served a higher-order value (e.g., loyalty to the boss).|
|**Metaphor of the Ledger**|Arguing that past good deeds or hard work entitle one to a "deviant" act.|

## Conclusion

Ethical decision-making in business is a multi-dimensional process. While individual moral development and personal integrity are vital, they are frequently challenged or suppressed by organizational contexts. Bureaucracy, reward systems, and the "moral muteness" of managers can create environments where unethical choices are rationalized as "just business." Effective management of business ethics requires identifying these points of leverage and fostering a culture that encourages moral imagination and protects those who exercise personal integrity.
